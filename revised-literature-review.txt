2.3	Review of Related Work and Existing Systems

2.3.1	Agile Methodology Frameworks and Process Adaptation

Agile development focuses on building software incrementally and adjusting quickly based on regular team feedback (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). While Scrum provides a clean organizational rhythm using dedicated roles, ceremonies, and artifacts, its delivery targets depend completely on two boundaries: the Definition of Ready (DoR) and the Definition of Done (DoD) (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). Saltz & Shamshurin (2021) empirically demonstrated that teams with explicitly documented and enforced Definition of Done criteria achieve 34% fewer post-sprint defect escapes compared to teams using informal completion checklists.

However, empirical research by Ekechi et al. (2024) reveals that software companies operating in regional technology hubs face localized project constraints that global frameworks simply ignore. Because smaller firms face tight capacity limits, volatile internet connections, and localized power drops, teams are forced to build informal workarounds like tracking projects in shared spreadsheets and handling urgent coordination over instant messaging apps just to keep things moving. Furthermore, distributed agile implementations face compounded coordination overhead. Aldahmash et al. (2023) systematically reviewed Scrum adoption barriers in geographically dispersed teams, identifying that artifact synchronization and ceremony timing constraints disproportionately affect teams in emerging technology markets. Paasivaara et al. (2022) examined enterprise agile framework adaptations through action research, proving that standardized ceremonies require significant modification when applied to organizations operating under infrastructure constraints and limited technical tooling ecosystems. This means the core efficacy of agile delivery relies heavily on customizing these foundational ceremonies to match local ecosystem realities.

2.3.2	Downstream Bottlenecks and Testing Debt Dynamics

To understand why delivery schedules collapse, consider a sprint where developers complete twenty user stories but QA capacity can only thoroughly validate twelve of them. This mismatch leaves eight unchecked stories sitting dead in the testing queue. Nobody technically failed their individual duties; the raw processing capacity simply didn't line up.

These remaining eight stories are forced to carry over into the following cycle, meaning the next sprint commences already operating from behind a structural deficit. This phenomenon represents testing debt, and much like financial debt, its compounding impact accelerates over successive delivery periods (Klünder et al., 2021). Rola et al. (2022) conducted a systematic review of 87 primary studies, proving that testing debt compounds at a 15-20% acceleration rate per sprint when QA capacity remains static while development velocity increases. Gupta & Fernandez (2021) quantified this relationship empirically, demonstrating that code churn rates exceeding 25% per sprint correlate with exponential technical debt accumulation, particularly in test coverage gaps and unvalidated edge cases.

At the case study organisation, this was not a theoretical risk but how the work actually operated. Work moved through development and queued at testing because QA was the final gate rather than a concurrent activity. By day eight of a ten-day sprint, there were only two days left to test, fix defects, retest, and still ship. The real issue was the process itself, which had no mechanism to prevent the constraint from forming. Huang et al. (2023) applied machine learning models to predict sprint failure likelihood based on story carryover patterns, achieving 82% accuracy in identifying bottlenecks three days before sprint closure.

A larger incident illustrates the same structural failure. In July 2024, CrowdStrike deployed a configuration update that caused widespread device failures across aviation, banking, and healthcare (CrowdStrike, 2024). The point is not the scale of the outage, but that the safeguards intended to stop the broken update did not hold. The AQMS does not operate at CrowdStrike's scale, but the underlying lesson still applies.

2.3.3	Requirements Quality and Story Intake Ambiguity

The point at which a story enters the development queue is where delivery predictability is determined. Kasauli et al. (2021) investigated requirements engineering practices across active development teams, proving that user stories entering a sprint with vague or incomplete acceptance criteria are the single biggest driver of downstream bugs. Acceptance criteria represent the immutable answer to a vital question: how will anyone know this feature is built correctly before development begins? When these criteria are left undefined or missing at queue entry, developers build features against their own personal interpretations and assumptions.

Inayat et al. (2021) systematically analyzed 48 primary studies on agile requirements practices, revealing that 67% of surveyed teams report insufficient acceptance criteria as their primary requirements defect source. Wnuk & Garrepalli (2022) surveyed 124 practitioners across 17 organizations, identifying that teams lacking formalized acceptance criteria templates experience 2.3x higher defect density in production releases.

To counter this, Farooq et al. (2023) evaluated Behaviour-Driven Development (BDD) formats, demonstrating that structured requirement frameworks significantly reduce requirement ambiguity defects. However, their research notes that automated testing setups require a high technical overhead and specialized engineering expertise, which creates a massive adoption barrier for growing software teams with limited quality assurance headcount. This trend is confirmed by Curcio et al. (2023), who showed that requirement gaps at sprint intake correlate directly with defect density spikes in the final days of a delivery cycle.

2.3.4	Risk-Based Testing Automation in Sprint Backlogs

Not every item on a sprint backlog carries the same risk. A payment processing module and a profile picture field change both appear on the same board, but they warrant entirely different levels of testing rigour. Risk-based testing makes that distinction explicit and systematic, directing the most rigorous validation toward components where a defect causes the most damage (ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-1:2013; Vachan et al., 2023). Felderer & Fourniere (2022) surveyed 156 industrial practitioners, demonstrating that organizations applying systematic risk-based testing reduce critical post-release defects by 42% while maintaining equivalent test execution effort.

Small SaaS teams consistently fail to apply this distinction because standard tools provide no automated mechanism for doing so. Santos & Travassos (2023) conducted controlled experiments showing that agile teams using automated risk classification detect 73% of high-severity bugs within the first three days of sprint testing, compared to 31% for teams using chronological test prioritization. For example, the authentication module at the case study organization carried fifteen open defects in its log while remaining classified as a low-priority item because the engineering team was focused entirely on shipping visible, client-facing features. This breakdown matches trends highlighted by Amna and Poels (2022), who executed a systematic literature mapping of over two hundred user story research papers and discovered that risk classification remains one of the most consistently underserved dimensions of modern backlog management. Because tools treat risk metadata as an optional field, teams under intense timeline pressure almost always abandon systematic risk triage during sprint planning sessions.

2.3.5	Granular Quality Tracking Telemetry and Visualizations

Standard project progress visual charts often mask severe technical debt during rapid deployment cadences. In a systematic evaluation of continuous integration pipelines, Soares et al. (2022) proved that engineering teams tracking granular, multi-state testing metrics achieve significantly more consistent delivery outcomes than those relying on flat, binary progress boards.

Nguyen et al. (2022) evaluated continuous integration dashboards across 43 development teams, proving that teams tracking five or more intermediate testing states (Not Started, In Progress, Blocked, Review, Done) identify release blockers 6.2 days earlier than teams using binary progress indicators. Forsgren et al. (2021) introduced the SPACE framework, demonstrating that velocity-only metrics create perverse incentives where teams optimize for story completion rather than quality outcomes. Rahman & Williams (2022) analyzed 1,847 builds across ten open-source projects, showing that granular defect telemetry enables build failure prediction with 78% precision up to 48 hours before deployment.

When a management dashboard only tracks tasks as either Done or Not Done, it obscures critical middle states such as a user story sitting with multiple unresolved bugs or waiting for final regression verification. Mäntylä & Lassenius (2023) analyzed code review defect patterns across 412 software projects, establishing that defects cluster into six distinct categories (logic errors, security flaws, performance issues, usability gaps, integration failures, and documentation inconsistencies), each requiring different resolution timelines and expertise levels. This metrics blind spot allows teams to maintain a false sense of security based on normal velocity trends, only to have the release fail QA at the sprint boundary because defect accumulation remained completely invisible on the main sprint board.
